Build a useful child playlist by choosing a small set of videos you have watched, arranging a clear last item and deciding who controls the next selection. A normal YouTube playlist organizes videos but does not lock the child into them. If the child will hold the device independently, pair the list with a suitable approval experience or device control.

## Pick a purpose for this session

Start with an ordinary situation: a short drawing activity after school, one familiar episode while an adult prepares lunch or a few videos about a topic the child asked about.

A purpose makes selection easier. You do not need a permanent library for every interest before the first session. Choose enough for the actual available time.

For example, a 15-minute window might fit an eight-minute tutorial and time to try the activity. Filling all 15 minutes with playback may work against the reason you chose it.

## Review the full item

Check the video beyond its opening. Notice language, sudden scene changes, commercial segments and activities a child might imitate. Also check whether the version is from the expected creator.

A thumbnail and a channel's reputation are useful clues, but they do not describe every moment. If you cannot review a new item today, use a familiar one.

Make approval scope deliberate. Adding one video and approving a whole channel have different consequences for future choices.

## Give the list an ending

Put a short, familiar item last or choose one complete episode. Tell the child what marks the end before pressing play.

Turn off recommended-next-video [autoplay](/guides/stop-youtube-autoplay-for-kids), then test how your particular playlist handles its own next item. Sequential playlist playback is a separate behavior. Do not assume the autoplay switch guarantees the playlist stops where you expect.

Leave a little time between the final video and the next family activity.

## Choose how the child accesses it

For adult-led watching, a saved list can be enough because the adult keeps the device or remote. For YouTube Kids, [share eligible videos](/guides/share-youtube-video-with-child) or use its approval settings.

LittleLoop offers another arrangement on iPhone and iPad: the parent adds video links and the child uses a separate player with time and PIN controls. The parent still has to choose and review the material. The official YouTube embed may show platform ads, and LittleLoop is not affiliated with Google or YouTube.

## Keep the collection small enough to maintain

Remove videos the child no longer wants and revisit broad channel approvals. A short library is easier for another caregiver to understand.

Write down a simple rule, such as "choose one of these two episodes" or "finish after the drawing tutorial." This avoids making the second adult infer your intentions from a long list.

If maintaining an app-based library becomes more work than the viewing itself, the [no-app approach](/guides/no-app-ways-to-control-what-child-watches) may suit the family better. The value lies in the selection and stopping point, not in the number of saved videos.
