You can use YouTube without YouTube Kids through an eligible supervised account, a parent-selected player or an adult choosing videos on a shared screen. These setups allow different amounts of independence. Restricted Mode and device time limits can add controls, but neither means that every available video was approved by a parent.

## Start with the missing requirement

Why does YouTube Kids not fit? Perhaps a particular tutorial is absent, the child wants a broader range of topics or the television setup is awkward. Those are different problems.

For one missing video, first check whether parent sharing into YouTube Kids can solve it. For a broader catalog used regularly, a supervised account may be more practical. For occasional family viewing, an adult can select the item without setting up an independent child browsing environment.

## Use a supervised account for broader exploration

A supervised YouTube account provides content settings and parent controls for eligible children. It remains a larger catalog with room to explore.

Review content level, autoplay, history and available time-management features. Then test the actual child view. The [account comparison](/guides/youtube-kids-vs-supervised-account) explains what changes when leaving the smaller Kids experience.

Restricted Mode filters potentially mature material but does not identify everything your family may want to exclude. It is not an individual-video approval system.

## Use a selected library for narrower choices

Approved-video players can let a parent build a small collection from YouTube. Compare whether approval applies to an individual item, a whole channel or future uploads. Also check whether the child can search, request videos or leave the player.

LittleLoop is one such option on iPhone and iPad. A parent prepares the videos, and child mode presents that list with a PIN and time controls. Its official YouTube embed can serve ads, and LittleLoop is not affiliated with Google or YouTube. It does not provide a separate video catalog.

If continuous swiping is the concern, examine [Shorts feed controls](/guides/block-youtube-shorts-for-kids) before deciding you need a different app.

## Use an adult-controlled shared screen

Choose a video together, turn autoplay off and keep control of the next selection. A shared television can be easier to supervise than a small personal screen.

This approach is useful when the family watches occasionally and the adult is available. It becomes less convenient if the child needs independent access while several caregivers take turns.

The [no-app routine](/guides/no-app-ways-to-control-what-child-watches) gives a concrete way to make that arrangement predictable.

## Decide what independence is appropriate

A child who can request a video but cannot browse the catalog has a different role from a child using supervised search. Neither arrangement needs to be permanent.

Agree on how new videos are chosen and when the rule will be revisited. If the current setup requires constant interventions, change the selection process rather than repeatedly adding another warning.

A workable alternative has understandable boundaries on the devices the family actually owns.
