The useful alternatives to YouTube Kids fall into a few different groups: parent-approved YouTube players, supervised regular YouTube, a separate streaming library, or a shared-screen routine without a new app. Choose according to the problem you want to remove. Another YouTube player may simplify approval but still use YouTube's embedded playback. A different library changes the available shows. Watching together changes supervision. None of those changes automatically guarantees suitable content, no advertising and an easy stopping time.

## Name the reason you want to leave

If the problem is unpredictable recommendations, try YouTube Kids' Approve content yourself setting before moving services. It changes the selection model within the app you already use. If the problem is that your child wants educational material absent from its catalog, a supervised regular YouTube account offers broader access, with less precise control over each viewing choice.

| Your main complaint | A reasonable next experiment | The tradeoff |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Too many unwanted choices | A small approved-only library | Parent review takes time |
| Missing a particular YouTube creator | Supervised YouTube or a parent-approved player | Broader access or device setup to manage |
| Too much commercial content | A separate catalog, checked for its ad policy | Different shows and possible subscription cost |
| Watching never ends | One episode on a shared screen | An adult must keep the stopping agreement |
| Too many settings to maintain | Parent starts each video and keeps the remote | Less independent use |

A replacement app is not always the smallest useful change. If an autoplay setting or a shorter queue solves the problem, keep the setup that already works.

## Keep YouTube but change who chooses

Approved-only players give parents a narrower starting point than an open feed. Some approve individual clips; others approve channels or automatically follow new uploads. That difference matters more than whether the interface looks child-friendly.

WhitelistVideo describes channel allowlisting across desktop and mobile setups. SafeTube by AstroSafe combines a child library with parent controls. Tubsy describes approvals for videos, playlists and channels, including Android TV access. Kivvie focuses on channel approval, while Vidling describes approval for several content types and AI-assisted suggestions. These are provider-described capabilities, not results of our own device testing.

Kidube offers another model, combining its own catalog with assigned external content. Kovi's current site describes a forthcoming product rather than an established option you can rely on today. We could not verify enough current Cuppie documentation to make a confident recommendation. Our [comparison of parent-control video apps](/guides/apps-to-control-what-kids-watch) explains the differences and links to each provider.

LittleLoop belongs in this group too. Our team makes it for iPhone and iPad. Parents choose the content before handing over the child player. It uses the official YouTube embed, so ads may appear, and it is not affiliated with Google or YouTube. It does not replace Android or television controls. A family happy with YouTube Kids' own approval workflow may gain little by switching.

For older children, supervised YouTube is a separate compromise. It keeps more of ordinary YouTube's catalog and uses supervised content settings rather than requiring parent approval of every upload. Discuss what your child can do when something unexpected appears. See [using YouTube without YouTube Kids](/guides/safe-youtube-without-youtube-kids) for the practical choices.

## Switch to a different library

### PBS KIDS Video

[PBS KIDS Video](https://pbskids.org/apps/pbs-kids-video.html) offers a catalog of PBS children's programs rather than an open YouTube creator feed. That can be useful when your child wants familiar characters and you want a smaller pool of programs to review. Availability is geographically limited, so check your location and device first. It is not a way to import any YouTube clip your child asks for.

A finite catalog is easier to inspect than all of YouTube, but you still choose which shows fit your child. Review an episode, including its pace and themes, before making it the default after-school option.

### Netflix Kids

A [Netflix kids profile](https://help.netflix.com/en/node/114275) can separate a child's viewing experience from adult profiles and apply maturity controls. It is worth considering if your household already pays for Netflix and the catalog contains programs you want. Check the current catalog, plan terms and profile restrictions in your country. A kids profile does not mean you personally approved every show it can display.

Protect access to adult profiles as well. Otherwise the child-facing profile is a convenient starting screen, not a reliable boundary. Decide whether you want one episode or a longer session before playback begins.

Neither service replaces the whole YouTube catalog. That may be exactly the advantage, or a reason it cannot solve your particular problem. Do not buy a subscription until you have checked for the actual programs your family will use.

## Use the screen you already have

A shared television can be a good YouTube Kids alternative when an adult chooses the video and holds the remote. The screen is visible, the selection is deliberate and the child does not need their own browsing session. A television is not inherently filtered, so protect adult and guest access where the app supports it.

On an iPhone or iPad, Guided Access can keep a session inside one chosen app. It does not remove that app's search or recommendations. Choose the content first, then use the device boundary for its narrower purpose.

You can also keep a written list of approved episodes, let the child choose between two and start the selection yourself. This works best when someone can supervise the handoff. It works less well when the whole reason for independent viewing is that no adult is available. Our [no-app viewing guide](/guides/no-app-ways-to-control-what-child-watches) describes routines and their limits.

## Try one change for a week

Choose the single problem you most want to solve and test one replacement on the device your child actually uses. Keep track of unexpected choices, work required from you and what happens at the stopping point. Those observations tell you more than a long list of advertised features.

Keep the old subscription until you know how to cancel it and whether saved selections can move. You do not need a permanent answer today. You need a setup you can explain to another caregiver, use consistently and change when your child's needs change.
